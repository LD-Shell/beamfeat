    Statistical guarantees

    This page states, for each selection procedure, exactly what is guaranteed, under which assumptions, and what was measured. Every number here is reproduced by the committed test suite (tests/test_selection.py, tests/test_estimators.py) or benchmark scripts. Calibration figures come from 100 trials and are quoted with their standard error; read them at that precision rather than at the last digit.

    What "FDR control at level q" means

    Each candidate j carries its own null hypothesis H₀ⱼ. For the default screen, H₀ⱼ is that candidate j is independent of the target. A selection procedure rejects some of these nulls, and the candidates whose nulls it rejects are its discoveries: the selected features, which beamfeat also calls the screened or certified set. Rejecting a true null is a false discovery, a Type I error. With R rejections of which V are false, the false discovery proportion is FDP = V / max(R, 1), and the false discovery rate is its expectation over repetitions of the experiment, FDR = E[FDP]. A single run with FDP above q is consistent with control; a mean over trials above q is not. All documentation and tests in beamfeat use this definition.

    Under the global null, where every H₀ⱼ is true, V = R, so the FDR is the probability of rejecting any null at all. Controlling it at q then means that at most a fraction q of pure-noise fits select anything.

    Permutation selector (default)

    Statistic. |Pearson correlation| (regression) or eta-squared (classification) between each candidate and the target — a fixed function of the data, which is the condition for a permutation test to be valid. Statistics that re-tune themselves on the observed target (e.g. a cross-validated lasso penalty) violate exchangeability unless the tuning is repeated inside every permutation; beamfeat deliberately avoids them.

    P-values. Add-one estimator (1 + #exceedances)/(B + 1) (Phipson & Smyth, 2010): valid in finite samples, and never zero. The floor 1/(B+1) interacts with the multiplicity correction — Benjamini–Hochberg needs the leading feature to reach q/m, Benjamini–Yekutieli the smaller q/(m c(m)) — so B is auto-raised to twice the bound belonging to the configured correction (satisfiability plus headroom for one null exceedance), capped at max_permutations. The requirement binds hardest when few candidates reach the floor together: several tied signals relax the threshold by their count, a lone one carries it alone. If the pool is large enough that even the cap cannot meet it, the selector says so in warnings_raised and an empty selection should be read as an exhausted budget rather than an absent signal.

    Null semantics. Marginal independence: H₀ⱼ is that candidate j is independent of the target. A near-duplicate of a genuine signal is not independent of the target, so rejecting its null is a true discovery; de-duplication is performed by the search's redundancy threshold, not the error-control procedure.

    Multiplicity. The estimators (BeamFeatRegressor, BeamFeatClassifier, BeamFeatTransformer) default to Benjamini–Yekutieli, valid under arbitrary dependence; PermutationSelector used directly defaults to Benjamini–Hochberg, valid under positive regression dependence (PRDS). Engineered candidates share parents and are mutually correlated by construction, so PRDS cannot be taken for granted and the estimators take the conservative option. Over 100 pure-noise pipelines BH returned features in 6 trials against BY's 1 (FDP 0.06 and 0.01, benchmarks/selector_calibration.py), both under the nominal 0.10. At this trial count the exact paired test does not reject equal rates (p = 0.06): both corrections saw the same 100 datasets, and the one trial on which BY selected is among BH's six. Read it as a consistency check rather than as evidence that BH loses control here. A selector instance passed to an estimator keeps its own settings, so selector=PermutationSelector() runs BH at the instance's target_fdr; the estimator warns when its selection_correction or target_fdr disagrees with the instance's.

    Measured calibration (Gaussian designs, 300 rows, 25 candidates, 5 signals, 100 trials). BH controls the FDR at q·m0/m, here q·20/25, and realised 0.046 ± 0.008, 0.084 ± 0.012 and 0.161 ± 0.016 at nominal 0.05/0.10/0.20 against ceilings of 0.040/0.080/0.160. BY is stricter by a harmonic factor and realised 0.013 ± 0.005, 0.018 ± 0.005 and 0.046 ± 0.008. Power 1.00 throughout. benchmarks/selector_calibration.py derives each bound from the design and stops with an error if a bound falls below a realised rate's whole 95% interval, so these figures cannot drift from the behaviour.

    What the marginal null cannot see. A feature can be jointly essential yet nearly marginally independent of the target — a centred quadratic (c − 0.5)² contributes almost no marginal correlation until its linear complement is fitted. The marginal screen has almost no power to reject the null for such a feature, and where the feature is exactly marginally independent of the target, failing to reject is correct under that null. The cost is measurable on targets built from them (Friedman #1, over six draws: the screen never rejects the null for c, giving a screening-admissible ceiling of 0.874 ± 0.006 against a representation oracle of 0.960 ± 0.003, with the pipeline reaching 0.780 ± 0.013. Around 0.086 is lost to screening and 0.094 to the search. The null for c² is rejected on some draws and not others, since (c − 0.5)² expands to carry a little marginal signal through it.) Joint selection recovers such features but certifies nothing; that trade is the design.

    Knockoff selector

    Fixed-X (Barber & Candès, 2015), used when n > 2p: no distributional assumption on the features — deterministic engineered columns are fine. The guarantee requires the linear model with Gaussian, homoskedastic noise. The construction's exchangeability identities X̃'X̃ = X'X and X'X̃ = X'X − sI are verified to numerical precision by the tests, also after the centring the lasso statistic applies (the knockoffs are built orthogonal to the intercept as well, which takes one row more than 2p). On a near-singular design (smallest covariance eigenvalue below 1e-6) valid knockoffs of the dependent columns would equal the originals, and the lasso's tie-breaking would then favour the originals; constant and linearly dependent columns are therefore left out of the construction, get W = 0, and are named in a runtime warning. Routing and the n > 2p requirement count the retained columns.

    Statistic. The fixed-X guarantee holds for a statistic that sees the data only through [X X̃]'[X X̃] and [X X̃]'y. On the fixed-X path for regression beamfeat uses the lasso-path signed maximum, W_j = max(Z_j, Z̃_j)·sign(Z_j − Z̃_j), where Z_j is the penalty at which column j enters the path, computed from those inner products alone; a test checks that rotating the rows, which preserves them, leaves W unchanged. Versions up to 0.5.0 used the difference of cross-validated lasso coefficients there, whose folds look at individual rows, so the guarantee did not strictly cover it. On Gaussian linear models at q = 0.2 (200 trials each) knockoff+ realised 0.180, 0.172 and 0.135 with the path statistic against 0.151, 0.165 and 0.155 before, with power 0.96, 0.98 and 0.48 against 0.97, 0.99 and 0.82: both under the level, and the price of the valid statistic is power when columns are correlated (0.7 between neighbours in the third design). Model-X, which admits any statistic that swaps sign with the pair, keeps the cross-validated one.

    Model-X Gaussian (Candès et al., 2018), used only when n ≤ 2p: assumes jointly Gaussian features, which engineered features violate; a warning is recorded, and the permutation selector is recommended in this regime.

    The estimators surface every selector diagnostic — assumption strain, unsatisfiable configurations — through the warnings module, so an empty knockoff result reads as "could not have selected at this configuration" rather than "no evidence"; when knockoff+ selects nothing but offset=0 would have, the warning says so and names the trade-off.

    Offsets. offset=1 (knockoff+) carries the finite-sample FDR guarantee and realised 0.170 at nominal 0.20 over 100 trials; it is unsatisfiable with fewer than 1/q features, and a warning fires on narrow designs. offset=0 controls only a modified FDR and realised 0.260, above offset=1 as expected. (With the cross-validated statistic used until 0.5.0, 0.159 and 0.249.) Both move across numeric stacks, since knockoff construction depends on matrix decompositions; treat the ordering and the nominal bound as the claim, not the second decimal. benchmarks/selector_calibration.py regenerates both, and the committed tests pin the ordering rather than the decimals.

    Selective inference: why the estimators split their data

    The search retains candidates because they correlate with the target in its sample. P-values computed on that same sample are therefore optimistically biased, and the nominal FDR is not guaranteed for them. By default the estimators search on one half of the training rows and run selection on the other (selection_holdout=0.5), restoring the fixed-candidate-set premise. Measured end-to-end over 200 replicates at nominal 0.10 (500 rows, 6 input columns of which 2 carry signal through their product, noise at 5% of the signal's standard deviation, max_depth=2, beam_width=25): not one false discovery, a 95% upper bound of 0.015 on the true rate, power 1.000, no fallbacks; 60 global-null replicates selected nothing. That is a far stronger signal than the selector-level design above — the generating feature correlates with the target at 0.999 — so read it as an end-to-end check that the split does its job, not as a power measurement at the margin. benchmarks/calibration_study.py regenerates it. If the holdout is disabled, the estimator refuses to claim the guarantee (fdr_controlled_ = False).

    Constants fitted inside expressions (fitted_ops) are part of the search. Each is chosen inside the search, on the search rows, before selection sees the candidates, and the selection rows receive every expression with its constant fixed, as they receive its structure. The fixed-candidate-set premise therefore holds as before and the guarantee is unchanged. A fitted constant is one more way the search adapts to its sample, so with selection_holdout=None, where search and screen share rows, it adds to the optimism for which the estimator already declines the claim. On pure-noise targets (50 seeds, 200 rows, 5 columns, defaults otherwise) the fraction of fits with any discovery was the same with all four fitted operators as without: 0/50 for regression, 2/50 for classification. The four are on by default since 0.6.0; at that default the end-to-end study above gives the same result as without them (no false discovery in 200 replicates, power 1.000, nothing selected in 60 global-null replicates), as does the pure-noise pipeline stress (BH 6/100, BY 1/100).

    magnitude_slots changes which expressions the search keeps, not how they are tested. Its magnitude score is computed inside the search, on the search rows, as the score is; the expressions it keeps are built on and ranked like any other; and the selection rows receive the search's output as a fixed set of expressions, chosen without them. The premise of the screen holds as before and its guarantee is unchanged. Measured on 200 paired replicates per design at q = 0.1 (benchmarks/search_stress/null.py, the screened set), the share of fits with at least one false discovery was the same with the option as without on four null designs, 0.015 to 0.025, among them centred and Gaussian inputs and 200 columns on 150 search rows; on a planted x0*x1*x2 among centred distractors it was 0.025 against 0.020, one fit in 200, with a false discovery rate of 0.005 either way.

    Columns inside a term (input_test)

    The screen's null is that a term is independent of the target, so (x0*x1)/(x1 + x8) is a true discovery when y = x0*x1: x0*x1 carries it, however unrelated x8 is. A reader of the equation still takes x8 to matter. input_test=True tests each column inside each candidate that reads more than one: the column's rows are permuted, wherever it occurs in the expression, and the screen's statistic is recomputed. The null for a column is that it is independent of the target and of the candidate's other columns together; under it, with independent rows from one distribution, permuting the column leaves the distribution of the data unchanged, so the p-values are exact. The screen's step-up correction runs across every (candidate, column) pair of every candidate, so which pairs are tested does not depend on the screen's outcome. A screened term is dropped before parsimony when the test fails to reject the null for any of its columns; input_report_ lists every pair, with null_rejected marking the pairs whose null the test rejected.

    What it does not cover: a junk column correlated with a real one is not null in this sense, so its null can be rejected and the term kept. Testing it given the other columns needs a model of the column given the others (knockoffs or the conditional randomisation test), which this test does not assume. The screen's guarantee is unchanged and still covers the screened set.

    Measured on 20 seeds of y = x0*x1 plus 25% noise among 10 junk columns (500 training and 500 test rows), against the default: printed equations with a junk column 14 → 1, printed terms with one 23/45 → 1/28, held-out R² 0.9413 → 0.9416. With the junk x8 correlated 0.82 with x1, 10 → 3. On pure-noise targets the screen rejects no null with the option or without it, and the physics panel is unchanged.

    Parsimony within the screened set

    Three things can be wanted of the fitted equation: that it be short, that the guarantee cover the terms actually printed, and that it cost no selection rows. Any two are available together, and the choice is one parameter.

    parsimony="forward" (the default) applies greedy forward selection within the screened set and fits the compact subset. It is short and costs no rows. What it gives up is the second property: the subset is chosen on the rows selection already used, so the q-level guarantee certifies the set it was drawn from and not the terms the forward step retained. equation() says so on its own output whenever terms were dropped —

    y = 0.9999*(x0 * x1) - 0.003943   [1 of 25 certified terms; parsimony=None prints all 25]

    — and fdr_scope_ records the same thing for code, which never sees that string: "screened set" here, "printed equation" when the guarantee covers the returned features themselves. fdr_controlled_ says whether there is a guarantee; fdr_scope_ says what it is over. The full screened set with exact p- and q-values per candidate stays auditable in selection_report_. fdp_inflation_ reports |S|/|S'|, the factor by which pruning can inflate the realised false discovery proportion: the denominator shrinks faster than the numerator, so the count of false discoveries cannot rise but the proportion can.

    parsimony=None prints the screened set entire. The guarantee covers what is printed — the equation is the certified object, not a selection from it — and it still costs no rows. The price is length: on wide data the equation runs to dozens of terms. fdp_inflation_ is 1.0, because the printed set and the screened set are the same set.

    parsimony_holdout buys both, and pays in rows. It splits the selection rows again: screening and parsimony use the first part, and the resulting subset — fixed at that point, and so an ordinary fixed-candidate set — is re-tested on the part held back. Terms whose null the re-test fails to reject are dropped, and because the guarantee then does cover the printed terms, equation() prints no subset note. The cost is real: comfortable above a few hundred rows, unaffordable below roughly a hundred, where parsimony=None is the better choice. If a compact certified equation cannot be produced, the whole screened set is returned rather than a pruned subset — the pruned subset is the one thing a caller asking for certification did not want.

    Measured. benchmarks/parsimony_holdout/ calibrates the procedure over 48,000 fits: a global null, and y = a*b/c plus noise among 5 or 20 distractors, with and without a column correlated 0.9 with a, at signal-to-noise ratios 1 and 3 and with 300 and 1,000 rows, 200 replicates per cell, at q = 0.1 and 0.2, beside the default and parsimony=None on the same data. The figures below are means over replicates. Their Monte Carlo standard errors are in the study's report, and are at most 0.012 for a design pooled over its four cells.

    • In the marginal sense, the guarantee's, the printed equation's realised FDR stayed far below q under every setting. On the global null it was 0.009 to 0.018 at q = 0.1 and 0.021 to 0.029 at q = 0.2 (a term printed in 7 to 14 and 17 to 23 fits of 800), and on the planted and proxy designs no setting printed a term built only from distractors in 3,200 fits per level. The re-test added nothing measurable here. On null data it failed to reject the null for the screen's rare false discoveries, in 20 of 22 fits at q = 0.1, and the fallback to the screened set then printed them anyway, so the printed equation's error is the screen's own.
    • In the conditional sense, counting a printed term that uses any column outside the generating formula, such as a rider (a*b/c) * x7, no setting controls the rate and the marginal re-test does not lower it. It was 0.67 to 0.77 under every setting at a signal-to-noise ratio of 1, where the forward step retains 18 terms on average. At a ratio of 3 it was 0.42 to 0.44 at the default and 0.53 to 0.62 with the re-test: on its smaller share of rows the forward step retains more riders, and each rider is associated with the target, so the re-test rejects its null.
    • Power to print the generating term (0.85 to 0.93 at a ratio of 1, 0.99 to 1.00 at 3) and held-out R² (within 0.0033 of the default, paired) hardly moved under any setting.

    certify_test="conditional" re-tests each frozen term given the others rather than on its own, with a Freedman–Lane permutation test (Freedman & Lane, 1983): the target and the term are residualised on the other terms and an intercept, the target's residuals are permuted, the statistic is the partial correlation, and the screen's step-up correction is applied at the screen's level. The null for a term is that its coefficient is zero in the linear model of the target on all the frozen terms: given the others, it adds nothing linear. That null is relative to the frozen set, so a term standing in for a true one the set leaves out is not null under it, and rejecting its null is not a false rejection. The p-values are approximate. For a single term the test is the marginal permutation test and exact. With others present its validity rests on the errors of a linear model of the target on the other terms being exchangeable (independent and identically distributed, so of constant variance), and even then the residuals it permutes are estimated and only nearly exchangeable, so the p-values are close to exact rather than exact (Anderson & Robinson, 2001; Winkler et al., 2014). The test is for regression only, and the step-up correction inherits its own condition: Benjamini–Yekutieli holds under any dependence among the p-values, Benjamini–Hochberg needs positive dependence.

    Measured in the same study, at a signal-to-noise ratio of 3 it brought the conditional rate to 0.18 to 0.22, paired −0.21 to −0.26 against the default. In the fits where it rejected at least one null it printed 1.1 to 1.2 terms, with a conditional rate of 0.026 to 0.040 at q = 0.1 and 0.052 to 0.073 at q = 0.2, and power 0.98 to 0.99. Its weakness is the fallback. When the frozen subset is long and its terms nearly reproduce one another, no term adds anything given the rest, the test rejects no null, and the screened set is returned, which carries the screen's marginal guarantee only. That happened in 87 to 92% of fits at a ratio of 1 and in 18 to 26% at 3 (37 to 52% with 300 rows, under 1% with 1,000). certification_result_.n_selected is 0 on such a fit.

    The equation list. equations() and equations_ record the forward path through the whole screened set, one equation per number of terms, at every setting above. Each line is a subset of the screened set chosen on the rows screening used, the same kind of selection as the default fitted equation, so the guarantee covers none of them but the last, which is the screened set itself, and, under parsimony_holdout, the re-tested fitted equation. An equation picked from the list after reading it is not covered; the list says so in its last line.

    What the default costs

    The exchange above is stated rather than assumed elsewhere in this page, so it is measured here. benchmarks/parsimony_cost.py re-runs the affected studies at both settings and pairs them fit by fit; benchmarks/PARSIMONY_COST.md records which studies depend on the setting and why. Over 308 paired fits across four studies:

    study fits mean ΔR² (forward − None) worst ΔR² terms, None ÷ forward fdr_controlled_ disagreements
    end-to-end calibration 260 −0.0001 −0.0003 25.0× 0
    known-formula + stress suites 18 −0.0008 −0.0148 26.0× 0
    Feynman physics panel 24 −0.0009 −0.0111 30.6× 0
    Friedman #1 decomposition 6 −0.0095 −0.0192 3.6× 0

    Compactness costs about a thousandth of R² on average and at most 0.019 on a single fit, and buys an equation roughly twenty-five times shorter. R² is held out in every study but the end-to-end calibration, which fits and scores on the same rows. fdr_controlled_ never disagreed between the two settings on any of the 308 fits, and the end-to-end calibration figures above — empirical FDR, its upper bound, power, fallbacks, null selections — are identical at both settings to every digit reported.

    Pruning was also never observed to inflate the realised false discovery proportion. That is not a theorem and should not be read as one: |S|/|S'| is the only bound available, and it is weak. It is an empirical observation with a mechanism behind it — greedy forward selection ranks by predictive contribution, and a marginally null feature contributes nothing, so it tends to be dropped first rather than kept. On a selector-level stress where screening was made to reject many true nulls (Gaussian design, nominal q = 0.5, 300 trials), the screened set realised FDP 0.397 and the greedily pruned subset realised 0.000, with the proportion rising in none of the 300 trials.

    Association is not generalisation

    FDR screening bounds the expected share of selected features that are not associated with the target. It does not certify that the linear model assembled from them generalises: a true discovery can carry extreme leverage — a reciprocal or logarithm evaluated near its pole — that destabilises the least-squares fit at points close to the singularity. The estimators keep these claims separate with a post-fit check on the selection holdout: a negative held-out R² (regression) or held-out accuracy below the majority-class rate (classification) raises a visible DegenerateFitWarning naming the gap. Rank-based scoring deserves particular attention here, since rank transformation hides exactly the magnitudes that cause the problem.

    When no null is rejected

    When the screen rejects no null, the default (on_no_discoveries="empty") uses no constructed features: the model degrades to an intercept-only fit (training mean, or class prior for classification), fdr_controlled_ is False, and a visible NoDiscoveriesWarning is raised. "fallback" uses the unfiltered search output, also with fdr_controlled_ = False; "raise" raises. In every mode, check fdr_controlled_ before treating fitted features as statistically vetted.
